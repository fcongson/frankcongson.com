My design process has become significantly faster with AI. In the past, I would gather reference images into Figma and use them to narrow down the design I was looking for, before I could start thinking about what to mock up. Now I can throw those reference images at Claude, and we go straight to mockups and iterate quickly. It was also fun. I spent my time looking at photos of cameras. I love the look of vintage cameras and their tactile dials, and there were so many great options to <s>copy</s> steal from 😆.

> "Good artists copy; great artists steal." — probably Pablo Picasso

This post is about how I designed Glint, my raw-first iPhone camera app. Have a read of [the first post](https://frankcongson.com/blog/building-glint) if you want the whole story.

## The rule

**Function and form** — the app had to function like a camera, and its form had to say "camera" 📷.

## First, it had neither function nor form

Before I started any real design iteration, Claude had built a camera app that didn't feel like a camera at all. It looked like a form. The first run on my phone was a plain SwiftUI form over a live preview, with two toggles and two unlabelled sliders. My next prompt to Claude was the start of the design cycle.

> "I'd love to iterate on the stylings of the controls. It would be great to make it feel more like a real camera." — My prompt to Claude

Within minutes, it had yellow RAW and M chips, a 4:3 viewfinder, and monospaced ISO, SHUTTER, and APERTURE readouts. After a few more iterations, it had tap-to-focus and a histogram chip.

## Inspiration from a Leica

I wanted to improve the UX and the interactions for Glint's manual controls. The single slider wasn't very intuitive, and it made sense to follow the familiar way physical cameras handle those controls — specifically the Leica M.

> "**Jakob's Law** (also known as the Law of Familiarity) states that users spend most of their time on other sites and apps, so they expect a new product to work the same way as familiar ones" — [Laws of UX](https://lawsofux.com/jakobs-law/)

I gave Claude images for inspiration and quickly got mockups for several options to choose from. It was great getting those mockups from Claude. They were even interactive, so I could get a feel for the interactions. That alone would have taken me days in Figma.

What I took from a Leica M (the image above is an M10, but the controls are the same):

- **The distance scale ending in ∞** became the model for the NEAR to ∞ focus rail.
- **The A beside the ISO and shutter values.** Auto is a position on the dial. In Glint, any setting can be on A: ISO on A gives shutter priority, shutter on A gives ISO priority, both give program, and the ruler then sets exposure compensation.
- **The meter.** The ▶ ● ◀ LEDs with the offset in stops became the exposure meter in the top bar.

Of the mockups Claude presented, I chose C. It made the best use of space while still feeling familiar. The viewfinder fills the screen, the focus rail sits on the right edge (close to the image you're focusing on, while covering as little of it as possible), and one wheel adjusts the selected manual setting. I did like the twin dials in A, but turning those tiny dials on a touchscreen seemed difficult. The touch targets were too small, a limitation of touch interfaces. B just took up too much screen real estate.

## The iPhone's fixed aperture

My iPhone 15 Pro has no adjustable aperture, so the Leica's depth-of-field scale has no equivalent. Glint shows the lens's f-number read-only, because the only way to change it is to change lens. The iPhone 18 Pro now has an adjustable aperture, which is obviously a very good reason to upgrade — for research 😉.

## User testing and fast iterations

Once I had the Leica-inspired designs coded, built, and installed on my phone, it was time to do some real user testing. That user being me 🤝.

Here are the five improvements that came out of it. Each started as a mockup, then I tested it out on my phone to make sure the change improved the issue I was having.

### 1. The chips over the viewfinder were unreadable

On a bright scene, I noticed HIST, ZEB, PK, and MAG were hard to read. They were drawn straight over the viewfinder. When off, they were bare white text that disappeared against the brightness, and the Dynamic Island clipped some of them.

### 2. The settings I rarely change needed a home

Taking the chips off the viewfinder left a problem: where do the settings I don't change often go? Until then, some of them lived in touch-and-hold menus on PK and ZEB. Cameras solve this with a quick menu, plus custom buttons for the few things you toggle often.

Fuji, Canon, and Panasonic call theirs Q. Sony uses Fn. In the first mockup, the button was labelled Q. I asked Claude why, then asked for a different name. SET was one of the options Claude gave me, and I liked that it's shorthand for "settings". It also borrows no brand's label and fits Glint's all-caps labels. Its first version was nine tiles — tap one and its choices appear underneath.

</Section>

### 3. The viewfinder got smaller, so the mode switch moved

As soon as I used the tool row version on the phone, I noticed the viewfinder had got smaller. It was losing space to the tool row. SET moved to the top bar beside the format shown as plain text (the RAW chip looked out of place), and I needed somewhere else for PHOTO | VIDEO, so I asked for two places to put it.

</Section>

I decided on the left stack: AUTO | MANUAL over PHOTO | VIDEO, beside the shutter. It mirrors Sony's mode dial with its photo, movie, and S&Q collar underneath, and it gave the bottom strip back to the viewfinder.

### 4. Landscape was a mess

Rotating every label with the phone made chips overlap and left the focus rail along the top.

</Section>

<ImageCaption

I didn't mock this one up. I decided it on the phone. Controls don't rotate on a physical camera, so I did the same in my camera app. Only the focus rail moves, to the side you choose (a setting in SET), turned so ∞ is up.

### 5. SET wasn't obvious

The tiles worked, but tapping one showed its choices in a panel along the bottom, and nothing tied the two together.

> "I want to improve the SET UX. It wasn't obvious to me that the bottom row was the options for the currently selected setting." — My prompt to Claude

Claude had designed that menu, and I couldn't tell how it worked. If I couldn't tell, anyone new to the app would struggle too. Claude's answer was three options, as mockups.

I chose rows, the one where every choice is visible. Each setting names itself and shows every choice as a segmented control, like a digital camera's menu system. The expanding version needed a pointer arrow to tie its choices to the tile, and the drill-in version hides everything else. I didn't like the additional steps to find the setting I wanted. I just wanted to get to the setting fast.

## App experience enhancements

The design of Glint has come a long way in a short amount of time, and I wanted the app to feel polished. That meant improving the experience outside the app too, the way you get into it: how the icon looks on the Home Screen, and how the Glint control looks on the Lock Screen and in Control Centre. I took both through the same design loop as the controls, with additional design iterations for each.

**The app icon.** I wanted it to say "camera" at a glance and to work in light, dark, and tinted modes. It started life as a placeholder sparkle, then went through five rounds in one evening.

</Section>
